Shock Wave Propagation
in a Conduit with a Porous Wall

How does the internal geometry of a porous wall govern the transmission, attenuation, and reflection of shock waves? This research characterizes the mechanisms using high-speed schlieren photography and multi-point pressure measurements at the Technion.

Overview

The Problem

When a shock wave travels along a conduit fitted with a porous wall, it creates an immediate pressure jump at the wall's front face. A weaker transmitted shock emerges from the rear face and propagates into the cavity behind, while fast mass flow drives strong vortex formation.

Over time, the cavity pressure rises until it equalises on both sides of the porous medium. During this transient process, the shock inside the cavity evolves through four distinct stages before both shocks settle into a coupled pseudo-steady propagation.

The central question is: how does the internal geometry of the porous wall — pore shape, orientation, and arrangement — govern this entire process?

Key Finding

Four Stages of Shock Evolution

High-speed schlieren imaging reveals a consistent four-stage transient process before pseudo-steady propagation is established.

Stage 1

Regular Reflection

The transmitted shock enters the cavity at a shallow angle and reflects regularly off the cavity floor.

Stage 2

Mach Reflection

The reflection angle steepens, the regular reflection can no longer be sustained, and a Mach stem forms and grows.

Stage 3

Wave Convergence

As the Mach stem reaches the porous wall, two shocks travel above and converge — the trailing shock overtakes the leading one.

Stage 4

Pseudo-Steady State

A perpendicular bottom shock and two coupled upper shocks propagate at a constant, locked velocity.

Experimental Setup

Shock Tube & Schlieren System

Experiments are conducted in a 6-metre square-section shock tube fitted with a fast-opening valve and a custom 1.2 m rectangular test section. The test section has clear PMMA windows that allow schlieren optical access, and accommodates interchangeable porous wall samples at adjustable stand-off distances.

A high-speed Phantom camera captures up to 20,000 fps synchronized with an Nd:YAG laser producing 240 ns pulses, giving temporally resolved schlieren images of the evolving shock patterns.
